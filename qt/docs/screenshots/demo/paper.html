Sampling and aliasing in practice

M. Keller · J. Duarte
Institute for Signal Processing
Abstract. We revisit the sampling theorem with measurements from low-cost data loggers and show how anti-aliasing filters of modest order already keep the aliasing error below the quantisation noise. A simple rule for choosing the sampling rate from the filter's order is derived and checked on three data sets.

1 Introduction

A band-limited signal is determined by its samples when it is sampled at more than twice its highest frequency. In practice signals are never strictly band-limited, and the question is not whether aliasing occurs but how much of it can be tolerated. This paper looks at that question with the instruments found in teaching laboratories.

Section 2 recalls the theorem and the effect of a non-ideal anti-aliasing filter. Section 3 describes the data sets, and Section 4 compares the measured aliasing error with the prediction.

2 The sampling theorem

Let x(t) have the spectrum X(f), and let it be sampled every T seconds. The spectrum of the samples is the sum of copies of X(f) shifted by multiples of 1/T. When X(f) vanishes above 1/(2T), the copies do not overlap and x(t) is recovered by an ideal low-pass filter.

A real filter of order n attenuates by about 6n dB per octave above its cut-off. The copies therefore overlap a little, and the overlap folds back into the band of interest as aliasing error.

3 Data sets

We recorded vibration, audio and temperature signals with three loggers sampling at 1, 8 and 48 kHz, each with and without an analogue filter in front of the converter. Every set was also recorded at a rate eight times higher as a reference.

4 Results

With a fourth-order filter the aliasing error stayed below the quantisation noise of a 12-bit converter whenever the sampling rate was at least 2.6 times the cut-off frequency. Without a filter, the error reached 4 % of the signal's power for the vibration data.
